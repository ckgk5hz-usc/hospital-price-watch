What other researchers find

RAND's national hospital price study (Round 5.1) reprices private claims at Medicare's rates. It found private payers at 254% of Medicare in 2022 and placed California among eight states above 300%. That study uses claims from employers who chose to take part and from 12 state claims databases, none of them California's, and covers about 6% of U.S. commercial hospital spending. RAND also found more of the price differences were explained by hospital market power than by a hospital's share of Medicare or Medicaid patients.

To go further: Sage Transparency shows RAND's hospital-level prices with quality; the NASHP Hospital Cost Tool shows what hospitals spend and what they would need to break even, from Medicare cost reports; Medicare Care Compare shows each hospital's quality measures.

How to read these numbers

  • Adjusted. A stay is adjusted for how complex the patients were (case mix), and a visit for how intensive it was, so hospitals treating sicker patients are not simply shown as pricier. The adjustment is imperfect.
  • Estimated from financial reports. HCAI estimates these from what hospitals report each year, not from individual claims. "Commercial" is HCAI's "other third party" category.
  • A different yardstick from RAND. RAND's national study reprices the same services at Medicare rates and found private payers at 254% of Medicare in 2022. This page divides average payments per adjusted unit, so the two should not be compared directly.
  • Who is left out. Kaiser hospitals and other facilities HCAI treats as non-comparable are not in the file. Psychiatric, rehabilitation and other specialty hospitals are removed here. A stay figure needs at least 100 stays for that payer in the year (100 for Medicare too), and a visit figure 1,000 visits; 236 hospitals have a commercial stay figure.
  • "Unusual" labels. A ratio below 0.75x or above 5x is labeled. It may be real, or it may reflect how a hospital reported its revenue or how many patients it had. Check before relying on it. This page does not rank hospitals from highest to lowest for that reason, though you can sort any column.
  • OHCA high-cost list. The label marks the hospitals the state's Office of Health Care Affordability identified using 2018 to 2022 data under its own definition. A JAMA Health Forum commentary questioned the list because pandemic years and data anomalies affected it.
  • Prices and quality. A higher payment does not by itself show a hospital is overcharging; costs, patient mix and local competition differ. Stars are CMS's single summary of mortality, safety, readmission, patient experience and timely care, and they are built from reported measures. 229 hospitals here have a rating; others had too few reported measures or no linked CMS number. Linking uses California's official facility listing, not name matching.
  • Critical access hospitals. Medicare pays these small rural hospitals based on their costs, so their ratios are not comparable with other hospitals'. 31 are in this table.
